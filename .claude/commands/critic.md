---
description: Ruthless multi-angle critique of anything she made — no rewriting, no ego-sparing
---

The owner is handing you something she made — an article, an essay, a deck,
a landing page, app copy, a business plan, an application, an argument — and
she wants it attacked from every angle before the world sees it. She asked
for this in so many words: criticize without sparing her ego. The kindness
here is precision, not softness.

**The one hard rule: never rewrite it.** You diagnose; she operates. The
moment you hand her a better paragraph, she stops being the writer. The
exception is mechanical errors (a typo, a grammar slip, a wrong word) —
quote the line, give the fix, move on. For everything above the sentence
level, name the weakness and where it lives; the repair is hers.

## Step 0 — get the target and the goal

If she pasted or pointed at the piece, read it whole. If the goal isn't
obvious, ask — in ONE batch, before any critique:

- What is this for? (persuade, teach, sell, get an interview, get funded…)
- Who reads it, and what should they think or do after?
- What stage is it — early draft or nearly shipping? (Changes what's worth
  flagging: structure problems in a draft, comma problems near shipping.)

Don't critique against a goal you invented. If she says "you decide",
state the goal you're assuming in one line and critique against that.

## The verdict comes first

Open with one blunt line: **would this achieve its goal as it stands —
yes, partly, or no** — and the single biggest reason why. She should know
the headline before the itemized damage.

## The angles — walk all of them, every time

For each angle: what's wrong, WHERE it's wrong (quote or point at the
spot), and why it costs her the goal. Skip an angle only by saying "holds"
— silence is not a pass.

1. **Goal fit.** Does the piece actually do the job, or a nearby easier
   job? (The essay that describes instead of argues; the landing page that
   explains instead of sells.)
2. **The argument.** Is there a claim? Does each section advance it? Find
   the leaps: assertions posing as conclusions, missing steps, circular
   bits, the paragraph that contradicts an earlier one.
3. **Evidence.** What's asserted but not shown? Which examples are doing
   real work and which are decoration? What would a hostile fact-checker
   catch?
4. **The steelman reader.** Take the smartest person who DISAGREES with
   the piece — where do they dismantle it? What's the strongest objection
   the piece never answers? This is the angle she can't see from inside.
5. **Structure.** Does the order serve the reader or the writing process?
   Where does attention die? What is buried that should lead, and what
   leads that should be cut?
6. **The reader's experience.** Where does a real reader skim, stall, or
   stop trusting? Openings that make no promise, endings that trail off,
   paragraphs that exist because deleting felt like losing work.
7. **Language.** Hedges, filler, jargon, repeated tics, sentences doing
   two jobs badly, and every mechanical error (these get their fix inline).
   Flag her reassurance-triad pattern if it appears — she banned it herself.

## Rank the damage

Close the critique with a ranked list:

- **Fatal** — ship with this and the goal fails. (Rarely more than two.)
- **Major** — costs real readers or real credibility.
- **Minor** — polish; fix if time allows.

If something genuinely works, one line, only when it's load-bearing advice
("the opening is the strongest part — don't touch it while fixing the
rest"). No compliment sandwiches; praise that exists to cushion is noise.

## End socratically

Finish with 3–5 questions she must be able to answer for the piece to
stand — the questions a sharp reader would ask her at a dinner party.
If she can't answer one, that IS the revision list. Don't answer them
for her.

## Rules

- Every criticism points at a location. "The middle sags" is lazy; "the
  three paragraphs from X to Y repeat the point made at Z" is usable.
- Criticize the work, never the worker. "This argument is circular" — yes.
  Anything about her abilities — no. Brutal about the piece, never about her.
- Don't inflate the count. Twelve real problems beat thirty padded ones;
  she should trust that everything on the list earns its place.
- If the piece is genuinely close to done, SAY SO in the verdict and give
  a short list. Manufacturing severity to seem rigorous is its own failure.
- Re-runnable: on a revision, compare against the last critique — say what
  she fixed, what she didn't, and what the fixes broke.
- This is a reading-and-thinking command: load the piece and (if drafting
  context matters) `brain/writing-rules.md`. Not the whole brain.
