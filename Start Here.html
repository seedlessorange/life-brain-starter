Life brain

A second brain for your life admin: every project and deadline you are carrying, with Claude Code doing the upkeep so you never have to.

What it is

A folder, a page, and Claude

The whole thing is a folder of ordinary text files, a page in your browser that renders them, and Claude Code doing the filing. There is no account to create, and if you one day abandon the tooling, the files still read as plain English.

What makes it different from a to-do app is that it answers the three questions a to-do app leaves you to work out alone.

What is quietly decaying?

Deadlines gone past, things untouched for weeks, things never started. The page flags them before they become emergencies.

Whose court is the ball in?

What is on you, what is on someone else, and who has gone quiet long enough that it is time to chase.

What deserves your next free hour?

A short ranked list, with a reason for each item, instead of a wall of two hundred tasks.

The daily rhythm

Your whole job is three things

  1. Drop thoughts in. Type them into the box on the page, any time, in any order. No categories, no tags.
  2. Tick things off as you do them.
  3. Answer a question when the brain asks one.

Everything else is Claude's job: sorting your notes into the right place, re-ranking what matters, chasing dates, rebuilding the page. Each morning it can write you a plan of three achievable tasks. That division of labour is the point of the design: a second brain that needs discipline from a busy person is dead in three weeks.

It also drafts for you. A task like "email the landlord" comes back with the email already written, waiting for you to read and send. Sending is always your click; the brain never sends anything itself.

Setting it up

Twenty minutes, mostly downloads

You need the life-brain zip you were sent, a free install of Python, and a Claude account (the Pro plan is enough) with Claude Code. You do not need to know anything about terminals; the two places a terminal appears, the steps tell you exactly what to type.

On a Mac

  1. Put the folder somewhere permanent

    Double-click the zip, then move the folder it makes into Documents. Don't leave it in Downloads; some Macs clean that folder out, and this one becomes your actual notes.

  2. Open Terminal at the folder

    Press ⌘ Space, type Terminal, press Return. Type cd  (with a space), drag the life-brain folder from Finder into the window, and press Return. Terminal is now pointed at your folder. Leave it open.

  3. Let macOS trust the folder

    macOS blocks files that arrived from the internet until you say otherwise. Paste this line into Terminal and press Return:

    xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine .

    Nothing visible happens. That is success.

  4. Check Python, then open the page

    Type python3 --version. A version number means you are done. A pop-up offering developer tools means click Install and wait; that gets you Python and Git in one go.

    Then double-click Open Brain.command in the folder. Your browser opens your brain page. Leave the small Terminal window it spawns open while you use the page.

  5. Install Claude Code and sign in

    Follow the Mac instructions at claude.com/claude-code. Then, back in your Terminal window, type claude. The first run walks you through signing in with your Claude account, once.

  6. Build your brain

    With claude running, type /onboard and press Return. Then talk: your projects, the people you owe replies to, the deadlines you are carrying, in whatever order things come to you. It sorts them out; there is no format to learn. When it finishes, refresh your browser. Your brain is there.

On Windows

  1. Put the folder somewhere permanent

    Unzip to Documents\life-brain. Don't leave it in Downloads; some Windows setups clear that folder, and this one becomes your actual notes.

  2. Install Python

    Download it from python.org/downloads and run the installer. On the first screen, tick the box that says "Add python.exe to PATH". It is at the bottom and easy to miss, and it is the single most common reason setup fails. If you missed it, run the installer again and choose Modify.

  3. Open the page

    Double-click Open Brain.bat in the folder. A black window opens and your browser shows your brain page. Leave the black window open while you use the page; closing it just stops the page, nothing is lost.

  4. Install Claude Code

    Follow the Windows instructions at claude.com/claude-code.

  5. Open a terminal in the folder and sign in

    In File Explorer, open the life-brain folder, click the address bar at the top, type cmd, and press Enter. A black window opens, already pointed at the folder. Type claude; the first run walks you through signing in with your Claude account, once.

  6. Build your brain

    With claude running, type /onboard and press Enter. Then talk: your projects, the people you owe replies to, the deadlines you are carrying, in whatever order things come to you. It sorts them out; there is no format to learn. When it finishes, refresh your browser. Your brain is there.

The rest of it

What else it can do

Most of this is already there the first time the page opens. The few that reach outside your machine — your chats, a Telegram bot, your calendar — take a couple of minutes each; the Connections button on the page lists them with live status, and asking Claude in the folder sets any of them up.

A plan that fits the real week

Give a task a deadline and a rough size, and the week answers honestly: what fits, what has to move, what will be late whatever you do. Your calendar and the weather where you actually are come into it, so the outdoor job lands on the dry afternoon.

Friendships get the same care as projects

You give each person a rhythm (weekly for your mum, monthly for an old friend) and the People page surfaces whoever has drifted past it, who you owe a reply, and whose birthday is coming. Bridge your chats through the free Beeper app and the last-spoke dates keep themselves true, from chat names and dates only, never the messages.

Rooms, and a finish line in each

Your life groups into rooms — one per project, one per front — each with its own notes and its own goals with dates on them. A goal that slips makes its whole room shout, exactly the way a late deadline does.

The season you are in

A bucket list for this stretch of life, with the months left drawn out in front of you. Drag an item onto a day and it becomes a plan; if it names people, the invitation is already written and waiting for you to send.

Tell it the day

Type or speak how the day went. Your words are kept exactly as you said them, then quietly farmed for the useful parts: who you spoke to, what got finished. The journal stays yours — the scheduled runs are blocked from opening it. Habits are counted for the week, with no lecture attached.

A briefing you would actually read

A news page built from outlets you pick and filtered to the subjects you follow. For a field you are learning, each morning adds a plain-language breakdown and one term worth knowing, which collects into a glossary of your own.

Text it from the bus

A Telegram bot, set up in two minutes. A thought sent from anywhere lands in your inbox, "plan" answers with today's list, and a voice note is transcribed on your own machine and turned into tasks.

An assistant with a queue

Type an ask on the page in plain words: "chase the letting agency and draft the email for me". Next run, Claude works through the queue and each card gets a note saying what it did.

Your Claude Code conversations, on the page

The Sessions page holds a live, resumable Claude Code conversation for each of your projects, several at once, so you can run them from the browser instead of a stack of terminals.

Your whole life as one picture

The map shows every workstream as a dot, clustered by area of your life and coloured by what is wrong with it: red for late, amber for someone owing you a reply, blue for going cold.

It watches your project folders

Point it at any folder with a TODO.md and the page picks up the open items every twenty minutes, along with how long since anyone worked there. Your projects show up without you filing anything.

Mornings and nights run themselves

At 7:00 it syncs, writes the day's plan, and notifies you only if something is genuinely overdue. The heavy jobs can run at 1am instead, in their own usage window, finished before you wake.

The page comes in six looks — a warm workroom, a printed broadsheet, a Bauhaus grid among them — and switching between them takes one click in the Appearance menu.

Your data

What it does with your life

Everything stays in the folder on your computer. The page is served at an address only your own machine can reach.

The brain drafts messages and emails for you, but you always press send yourself. Anyone you mark as family or a close friend is draft-only in the code itself: the brain cannot message them even if asked to.

The price

What it costs

Nothing beyond the Claude subscription you already have or would sign up for. Pro is enough: the brain defaults to the cheapest models for routine filing, and the heavy work can run at 1am in its own usage window, finished long before you wake.

It also keeps its own books. Every Claude job lands in a ledger, by day and by model, and a Usage page shows what each feature spends — so if a week feels expensive, you can see which part of the brain did it.

Made with a folder of markdown, a Python server, and Claude Code. When a newer copy reaches you, ask Claude to update in place: the code changes, your workstreams and people and settings stay exactly as they are. Once yours is running, it can build a clean copy to pass on to the next friend.
